The wet plate collodion process, start to finish

Make your first tintype in a single sitting. This guide follows the whole collodion sequence — which must happen in about 10–15 minutes, while the plate is still wet — from a clean plate to a varnished, finished image.

Your first tintype — full walkthrough 18:24

Wet plate collodion is a wet process: the plate is coated, sensitised, exposed and developed before the collodion film dries. Set up everything within arm's reach before you pour, and read the safety notes first — you'll be working with ether, alcohol and silver nitrate.

Safety first

  • Collodion contains ether and alcohol — highly flammable. Work with ventilation and keep all flames away while pouring.
  • Silver nitrate stains and is corrosive; wear nitrile gloves and eye protection.
  • Salting halides (cadmium salts) are toxic — don't inhale or ingest.
  • Fix with sodium thiosulfate (hypo), not potassium cyanide.

Written walkthrough

Step by step

The written version of the video. Once the plate is coated, you're on the clock — the whole sequence to the fixer should take about ten minutes.

Clean & prepare the plate

Start with a spotless plate — any grease or dust shows up as a defect. For a tintype, use a pre-blackened aluminium plate; for an ambrotype, clean glass polished with a little calcium carbonate and alcohol. Handle by the edges only. Have the plate ready before you mix or pour.

Flow the collodion

Hold the plate level on your fingertips. Pour a pool of salted collodion onto the centre, then tilt the plate so it flows to each corner in turn, and drain the excess back into a waste bottle from one corner. Aim for an even, streak-free film. Wait a few seconds until the surface turns tacky (it gels as the ether flashes off) — then move straight to the silver bath.

Sensitise in the silver bath

Under safelight (collodion is only sensitive to blue/UV light), lower the coated plate smoothly into the silver nitrate bath in one motion to avoid streaks. The silver reacts with the halides in the collodion to form light-sensitive silver iodide and bromide. Leave it 3–5 minutes, until the surface stops looking greasy and water sheets off evenly.

Load the holder & expose

Still under safelight, lift the plate, drain the corner, wipe the back, and load it into a wet plate holder. Carry it to the camera and expose while wet. Collodion is very "slow" (roughly ISO 0.5–1) and sees mostly blue light, so exposures run from a second or two in bright sun to many seconds indoors. Bracket generously on your first plates.

Develop

Back under safelight, hold the plate level and pour the iron developer across it in one sweep so the whole surface is covered instantly. The image rises in seconds — rock gently and watch. Develop for about 10–15 seconds; the moment the highlights look right, flood it with water to stop development. Over-development gives a flat, foggy plate.

Fix — the magic moment

Now you can switch on white light. Pour fixer (sodium thiosulfate) over the plate: the milky, unexposed silver halide clears away and the positive image suddenly resolves out of the murk — the moment that hooks everyone. Fix for one to three minutes until fully clear.

Wash & dry

Wash the plate thoroughly under gently running water for several minutes to remove all residual chemistry — poor washing causes the image to deteriorate later. Stand it on a corner to drain, then dry it slowly and evenly, warming gently from behind. Don't rush this with high heat or the film can lift.

Varnish to protect it

The collodion film is fragile, so seal it. Warm the dry plate, flow on warm sandarac varnish exactly as you flowed the collodion, drain the corner, and gently heat it again until it sets to a hard, glossy finish. Varnished properly, a plate lasts for generations.

Troubleshooting

Common first-plate mistakes

Streaks & ridges

Usually uneven flowing or a jerky dip into the silver bath. Pour and immerse in one smooth, continuous motion.

More:Knowledge base ↗

Foggy, low-contrast plate

Often over-development or an old, contaminated silver bath. Cut development time and test, then sun and filter the bath.

More:Knowledge base — troubleshooting ↗

"Oysters" & comet trails

Contamination or particles in the developer. Re-clean the plate; filter your developer and keep dust off the plate edge.

0:00 Intro & safety

"Before we touch any chemistry: collodion is ether and alcohol, so it's flammable — no flames near the pouring station, and keep the room ventilated. Gloves and goggles on. With that out of the way, let's lay everything out so it's within reach, because once we pour we won't have time to go looking for things."

3:00 Flowing the collodion

"Pour a generous pool in the centre — don't be timid. Now tilt to this corner, then this one, then this one, and drain off the last corner back into the waste bottle. You're looking for a clean, even sheet with no ridges. Give it a few seconds to go tacky before the bath."

10:40 Developing

"Plate dead level. One confident sweep of developer right across — you want the whole surface wet in under a second. Watch the image come up... highlights are building... and stop it with water. That's about twelve seconds. If it goes milky and flat, you went too long."

12:30 Fixing

"Lights on. Pour the hypo over, and watch — this is everyone's favourite moment. All that milkiness clears, and the picture just appears out of nowhere. Rinse it well, and we'll dry and varnish to make it permanent."
